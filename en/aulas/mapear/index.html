Lesson 3 · 20 min

Map what really happens

Ask three people how an order becomes an invoice and you hear three different processes, all prettier than the real one. The map that works for redesign comes from following real cases: the normal path, the detours, who decides what, and how long each thing takes. In this lesson you build that map on one page.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1The six blocks of a map that works for redesign
  2. 2Interview the owner and follow five cases
  3. 3Separate rule-based decisions from judgment-based decisions
  4. 4Measure volume, people-minutes, rework, and waiting

Objective: map the normal path, the detours, the decisions, and today's measurements of a process, from an interview and from cases you followed.

Scroll the page: each step below lights up its part of the diagram.

  1. 1/7

    Trigger

    What starts the process, through which channel, and from whom. "Customer sends the order on WhatsApp" is a trigger. "Sales" is not. If you can't name the trigger, you don't know where to start counting time.

  2. 2/7

    Normal path

    The steps in the order they happen when nothing goes wrong, each one with who does it and in which system. It's the tagging sheet from lesson 1, now with wait time and people-minutes on each line.

  3. 3/7

    Decisions

    Where the path splits. For each decision, the test: would two well-trained people, with the same information, always give the same answer? If yes, it's a rule and can become a system. If not, it's judgment and stays with a person.

  4. 4/7

    Detours

    The paths a case takes when something is missing or goes wrong: where it leaves the normal path, where it returns, and how often this happens. Rework lives in the detours, and it's where an agent that only copies the normal path gets lost the most.

  5. 5/7

    Measures

    Volume per week, people-minutes per case, time from trigger to completion, rework and the longest wait. Each number says where it came from: counted in a system, observed in the cases or stated by someone (hypothesis).

  6. 6/7

    Completion

    What comes out of the process, for whom, and the criterion that says it's finished. "Invoice issued and sent to the customer" is a completion. Without it, the process has no end, and cycle time can't be measured.

  7. 7/7

    The whole map

    Trigger, normal path, decisions, detours, measures and completion, on one page. It's the raw material for the redesign in lesson 4 and the baseline in lesson 6.

02 · How to map

Four steps, in one week. The Horizonte data is fictional, for study.

1. Interview the owner

The process owner is the person who answers for the result, not the one who does the most steps. Ask about the trigger, the completion, the decisions and the detours they know. Use the script alongside.

The interview gives you the process as the owner thinks it is. Write everything down, but don't draw it yet: following the cases will correct a good part of it.

At HorizontePatrícia answers for the order all the way to the invoice. In the interview, she described 9 steps. The cases we followed showed 13.

2. Follow five cases

Pick five real cases and follow each one from start to finish: time of each step, people-minutes, where it detoured. Prefer cases that differ from each other: a common one, one with a decision, one that went wrong.

Five cases are exploration, not a sample. They're for drawing the map and finding where to measure, not for stating an average to Carlos.

At HorizonteFrom 10/15 to 10/17, Patrícia followed five orders: a normal one, one with a discount request, one from a customer without a CNPJ, one with a product shortage and one above R$ 5 thousand. The numbers are in the "The five cases" tab.

3. Draw decisions and detours

Mark each point where the path splits. Write the decision's question and apply the test: would two trained people answer the same? A rule can become a system; judgment stays with someone, by name.

For each detour, note where it starts, where the case returns and what gets lost along the way: time, rework, customer waiting.

At Horizonte"Does the customer have a complete registration?" is a rule, but today it's only asked at invoicing, after picking. Case P3 came back from finance and took 75 hours to get to the invoice.

4. Measure today

Four measures: volume per week, people-minutes per case, time from trigger to completion and rework. Add the longest wait: almost always, process time is queue, not work.

Every number carries its origin: counted in a system, observed in the cases, or declared. A declared number enters as a hypothesis until it is counted (lesson 6).

At Horizonte168 orders in the week of 10/13 (dashboard). Median of 21 minutes of people time and 30 hours until the invoice in the five cases. Rework: "about 15 a week come back," says finance (hypothesis).

Simulation: illustrative example

Order via WhatsApp → invoice · v1.0 · owner: Patrícia · Horizonte, fictional study company

Trigger: customer sends the order via WhatsApp. Completion: invoice issued and sent to the customer in the conversation.

Normal path (13 steps): reply → enter in the app → copy to the spreadsheet → check the price of every item → approve above R$ 5 thousand → print and take to the warehouse → check availability in the ERP → pick → notify Juliana → email to finance → retype in the ERP → check the customer record and issue the invoice → send the invoice.

Decisions: discount within policy? (rule) · above R$ 5 thousand? (rule) · discount between 5% and 10%: give it or not? (Patrícia's judgment) · customer with a complete record? (rule, today only at step 12) · product out of stock: deliver partially or wait? (judgment, with the customer).

Detours: D1 incomplete record: finance sends it back to customer service after picking ("about 15 a week," declared = hypothesis). D2 product out of stock: partial order, Juliana calls the customer (1 in 5 cases, observed).

Measures: 168 orders in the week of 10/13 (counted, dashboard) · 21 min of people time per order (median, 5 cases) · 30 h until the invoice (median, 5 cases) · longest wait: finance invoices the next morning, and the warehouse receives the papers in two batches (10 am and 3 pm).

  • A map the way the owner told it. If the map has fewer steps than the cases showed, you drew the interview.
  • Only the happy path. Without at least two detours, the map hides where the rework is.
  • An average of five cases sold as truth. Five cases show where to measure; the number for deciding comes from lesson 6.
  • A number with no origin. "About 15 a week" is declared: it enters as a hypothesis.
  • Personal data on the sheet. Use a code for the case, not the customer's name and phone.

06 · Summary

Six blocksTrigger, normal path, decisions, detours, measurements, conclusion.
Cases, not opinionAn interview gives you the hypothesis; five followed cases correct the map.
Rule or judgmentSame information, same answer: rule. Otherwise, it stays with a person.
A number with a sourceCounted, observed, or declared. The time is almost always in the waiting.
