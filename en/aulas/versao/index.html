Lesson 7 · 15 min

A process with a version

At Horizonte, the process changes when someone complains loudly: a scare created the check of every order, and nobody wrote down when or why. Software does not change like that. It has a version number, a record of each change, and a tested way to go back. In this lesson you treat the process the same way and record the move from version 1.0 to 2.0.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1Give the process a version number
  2. 2Turn a complaint into a proposal, not a change
  3. 3Promote step by step, with approval from whoever is accountable
  4. 4Write the trigger and the way back first

Objective: record the process change v1 → v2 with the reason, evidence, approver, phased rollout, and the way back, the way it is done with software.

02 · Versioning in practice

Four rules. Horizonte is a fictional study company; the dates and decisions in the example are fictional too.

1. Number and history

Give the process a number. A change that cuts, moves, or merges a step bumps the whole version (1.0 to 2.0). An adjustment that doesn't touch the steps, like changing the deadline for a reply, bumps the decimal (2.0 to 2.1).

Keep a one-line history per version: number, date, what changed, why, who approved. It's what answers, six months from now, why the process is the way it is.

At HorizonteThe check of every order went in on 10/14 with no record. In the new history, it shows up as part of v1.0, with the reason Patrícia remembered: the improper discounts that week.

2. A complaint becomes a proposal

One bad case is a signal, not evidence. It opens a proposal with the problem, the source cases, and the hypothesis. The change only goes in after it is tested and approved.

This protects the process from whoever complained loudest last, even when the one complaining is the owner.

At HorizonteA customer complained about an invoice that took three days. Instead of creating a new check, Patrícia opened a proposal: the source case is P3, and the hypothesis is to move the registration check to intake.

3. Promote a little at a time

The new version starts in the pilot scope, for a set period, with the indicators compared to the baseline. Only after that does it apply to all cases.

Promotion is a decision for whoever answers for the process, with the numbers on the table. The proposer presents; the one who answers decides.

At HorizontePhase 1, two weeks: v2.0 only for registered customers with orders up to R$ 5k. Phase 2: all orders. Patrícia proposes; Carlos approves each phase after looking at the three indicators.

4. Going back is written down

Before changing, write down when to go back (the trigger, with a number) and how to go back (the steps, who does them, how fast). Deciding this after the problem is deciding in a panic.

Rolling back is not failure: it's what lets you test without fear. What can't happen is the previous version having disappeared.

At HorizonteGo back to v1.0 if the billing returns stay above the baseline for two weeks in a row, or if an order goes out with a discount outside the policy. Patrícia decides within one business day; the spreadsheet and the paper come back the next day.

Simulation: illustrative example

Change no. 001 · WhatsApp order → invoice · v1.0 → v2.0 · Horizonte, fictional study company

  • Problem: orders come back from billing because of incomplete registration and go through 4 handoffs (cases P1 to P5).
  • Hypothesis: by cutting the handoffs, checking only exceptions, and moving registration to intake, the time to invoice and the returns drop, with no discount outside the policy.
  • Proposed by: Patrícia · approves: Carlos.
  • Evidence: four-week baseline counted in the app and the ERP before the change; test in phase 1, for two weeks.
  • Phases: 1) registered customers with orders up to R$ 5k; 2) all orders.
  • Rollback: returns above the baseline for two weeks in a row, or one order with a discount outside the policy. Patrícia decides within one business day. Steps: Juliana goes back to copying into the spreadsheet and printing; Rafael turns off the automatic handoff to the ERP; finance goes back to typing it in. v1.0 is kept in the process folder.

Fictional study dates and decisions.

  • Change with no number. If you can't say which version the process is on, you can't go back.
  • The proposer approves. Approval belongs to whoever answers for the process.
  • Vague trigger. "If it gets worse" is not a trigger; "returns above X for two weeks" is.
  • Previous version erased. Keep the v1.0 map, tagging sheet, and executor chart.
  • Changing everything at once. Phases let you see the effect before it applies to everyone.

04 · Practice

Horizonte's record is for practice; your process's record is for real.

1 · Horizonte's v1.0 → v2.0 record

20 min

Use the redesign sheet from lesson 4, the chart from lesson 5, and the indicator sheets from lesson 6. Fill in the change record and the history without looking at the tabs. Run the review prompt and fix what it points out.

Done when: the record has a problem with source cases, a hypothesis with an indicator and a guardrail, an approver different from whoever proposed it, 2 phases with a scope, 2 rollback triggers with a number and a deadline, and the steps back with who does each one; the history has the 1.0 and 2.0 rows.

2 · Your process record

30 min

Make the change record from the current version of your process to the redesigned one, and the history. Agree with whoever approves it before you start phase 1.

Done when: the record is complete, with the baseline from lesson 6 as starting evidence, the approver agreed in writing, the date for phase 1, and the current version saved somewhere another person can find it.
